Most important fixes:
1. **Chari (5).** Chari is not the "largest skin-tone-labelled rPPG study". VitalVideos has 893 subjects with Fitzpatrick labels (Tang et al. 2024). The 3.9 and 6.2 bpm figures belong to the facial-aggregation baseline, not to Chari's method. ECG was recorded but was not the reference.
2. **Dasari (7).** The Indian data were recorded with participants seated indoors, not outdoors.
3. **Ref 31.** Add the authors: Pstras, Okupnik, Ponikowska and Paleczny, with the DOI.
4. **rPPG-Toolbox (17).** The paper benchmarks 11 methods, not 12. The MMPD error range is about 9–18 bpm for supervised models trained on real data, and 12–22 bpm for unsupervised methods.
5. **Harris (12).** The guide describes conformal prediction. I found no point where it recommends it.
6. **Risk register.** Published Indian ITA° data suggest the ITA ≤ 10° stratum is the easiest to fill. The lighter stratum (ITA > 28°) is the scarce one. See the last section.

## Key claims

**Chari et al. 2020: MAE by skin type and the cause of the bias.** These figures are correct as numbers but are attributed to the wrong method. Table 2 of https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.12769 gives MAE for Light (FP 1–2), Medium (FP 3–4) and Dark (FP 5–6):

| Method | Light | Medium | Dark |
|---|---|---|---|
| Facial aggregation (baseline) | 3.94 | 4.14 | 6.20 |
| SNR weighting | 3.86 | 4.45 | 7.24 |
| Proposed method | 3.74 | 3.83 | 5.65 |

The text says: "The facial aggregation approach observes a performance of 3.94, 4.14 and 6.20 beats per minute". So "3.9 to 6.2" is the baseline, not Chari's method.

The "+2.06 from III–IV to V–VI" figure (power table, H1) does not appear in the paper. It is derived as 6.20 − 4.14 = 2.06, which is arithmetically correct; say "derived from Table 2". The lightest-to-darkest difference is 2.26 for the baseline and 1.91 for Chari's method, so the H1 assumption of 2.0 bpm remains reasonable.

The cause of the bias is confirmed. The paper says the error "is not due to biophysical factors, but instead due to imaging noise", and "the (biophysical) SIR is independent of the skin melanin content" while "the signal strength reduces with increasing skin melanin content". The proposal's phrase "relative size almost unchanged" paraphrases the signal-to-interference ratio (SIR) result. Better wording: "melanin lowers the signal reaching the sensor but leaves the biophysical signal-to-interference ratio unchanged".

**Verdict: CLAIM-MISMATCH.** Replace "In the largest skin-tone-labelled rPPG study, camera heart-rate error was 6.2 … against 3.9 for types I–II" with: "In a 54-person skin-tone-labelled study (VITAL), a standard facial-averaging pipeline's heart-rate error was 6.2 beats a minute for Fitzpatrick types V–VI against 3.9 for types I–II (Chari et al. 2020)." The larger VitalVideos study (893 subjects, https://arxiv.org/html/2404.05003v1) disproves the "largest" claim.

Also fix the §2.3 table. Change "No ECG or HRV; no compression" → "ECG recorded but not used as reference; no HRV; no controlled compression". The paper reports "aggressive compression" artefacts in its source video.

**Sjoding et al. 2020 NEJM.** Confirmed: "Black patients had nearly three times the frequency of occult hypoxemia" (11.7% vs 3.6% at Michigan; 17.0% vs 6.2% in the multicentre cohort). NEJM 383(25):2477–2478. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7808260. **Verdict: VERIFIED.**

**Dasari et al. 2021: the Indian dataset.** Confirmed: 44 Indian adults (25 F, 19 M), Samsung J7 smartphone, and a Masimo MightySat finger pulse oximeter as reference. The setting is wrong in the proposal. The protocol is to "ensure the subject is seated indoors and at rest, while facing the front camera of a mobile phone", so it was not outdoors. The Indian group is part of a 140-person study that also includes 96 participants from Sierra Leone. No per-subject skin tone was measured; only the pixel colour distributions of the two countries were compared. The paper concluded "skin tone may not be a critical factor impacting the bias". That counter-finding is worth acknowledging. https://sakthikap.github.io/files/npj_digital_medicine.pdf. **Verdict: CLAIM-MISMATCH.** In §2.2, replace "recorded 44 Indian adults outdoors on a smartphone" → "recorded 44 Indian adults, seated indoors, on a smartphone".

**Saikia et al. 2026: 119 videos.** Confirmed: "119 facial video recordings", from participants aged 18–40 (26 F, 93 M), with Indian demographics and authors at IIT Indore. The target is respiratory rate, using a respiration-belt reference, but a finger-oximeter pulse was also recorded. Skin tone is reported as a Fitzpatrick distribution, and the paper does not say how it was assessed. The weak results at the skin-tone extremes are confirmed. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.21511. **Verdict: VERIFIED with a minor wording fix** (see table row 30).

**Finžgar and Podržaj 2020: r = 0.53.** The abstract reports "r = 0.529 (p < 0.001)" for RMSSD on 60-s recordings. Table 5 lists it as a Spearman coefficient. The data are PURE (10 subjects, 56 recordings), and the reference is a finger pulse oximeter (PPG), not ECG. https://peerj.com/articles/8342.pdf. **Verdict: VERIFIED.** It would help to state the metric and context: "RMSSD from 60-s video, r = 0.53 against finger PPG, 10 subjects".

**2026 HRV pilot (ref 31).** The article is Pstras, Okupnik, Ponikowska and Paleczny, Sci Rep 2026, doi:10.1038/s41598-026-70292-0. The preprint says "r = 0.88 for lnRMSSD, P < 0.001". 35 of 38 recruited participants were analysed, all at rest, and the preprint states "we studied only white subjects". The reference was a 1-lead ECG (lead I), and no ICC was reported. The EMJ coverage of the Sci Rep version says: "The Pearson correlation coefficient reached 0.98 for SDNN and 0.88 for lnRMSSD". https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.02.13.25322028.full.pdf ; https://www.emjreviews.com/?p=563571. **Verdict: claims VERIFIED; the reference itself needs CORRECTED author details.** Caveat: the "white only" and "no ICC" details come from the preprint. I could not fetch the Sci Rep full text.

**rPPG-Toolbox (Liu et al. NeurIPS 2023).** The appendix gives these MMPD MAE values:
- Unsupervised: GREEN 21.68, ICA 18.60, CHROM 13.66, LGI 17.08, PBV 17.95, POS 12.36.
- Supervised, trained on UBFC-rPPG or PURE: TS-CAN 14.01/13.94, PhysNet 9.47/13.93, PhysFormer 12.1/14.57, DeepPhys 17.50/16.92, EfficientPhys 13.78/14.03. Models trained on SCAMPS reach up to 22.69.

The best clean-video result is 0.98 bpm (PhysNet trained on PURE, tested on UBFC-rPPG). There are 11 benchmarked methods, and nothing is stratified by skin tone. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.00716

So "about 1 bpm on clean video" is correct, but "12–17 on MMPD" for trained networks is too narrow. For unsupervised methods the range is 12–22 bpm, not 12–17.

**Verdict: CLAIM-MISMATCH.** Replace with: "trained networks reach about 1 beat a minute on clean laboratory video but 9–18 on the harder MMPD set (unsupervised methods: 12–22)". Also change "12 methods" to "11 methods". The statement that POS was "the most consistent unsupervised method across four test sets" is the proposal's own reading of the tables, not something the authors state. POS is best on PURE (3.67) and MMPD (12.36), and CHROM is best on UBFC-rPPG (4.06). Say "in our reading of the rPPG-Toolbox tables".

**MMPD (Tang et al. 2023).** All confirmed: 33 subjects, Fitzpatrick types 3–6, a Samsung Galaxy S22 Ultra phone, four lighting conditions, and HKG-07C+ finger-PPG as reference. Videos were "compressed to 320x240". The paper says: "for types 4, 5, and 6, most of the results showed a mean absolute error (MAE) greater than 10". ## Indian ITA° ranges (can the ITA ≤ 10° stratum be filled in Surat?)

- **Pawar et al. 2024.** *Facial skin color assessment and proposed skin shade guide for maxillofacial prosthesis using cluster analysis in a cohort of Indian adults.* J Indian Prosthodont Soc 24(4):366–373, doi:10.4103/jips.jips_194_24. They measured 368 Indian adults aged 20–45 at the malar (cheek) area with a SkinColorCatch colorimeter. Three clusters emerged:
  - "Dark": 88 people (23.9%), ITA −47.0 ± 14.8°
  - "Medium": 224 people (60.9%), ITA 4.3 ± 11.3°
  - "Light": 56 people (15.2%), ITA 34.7 ± 4.3°

  If the clusters are roughly normal, about 65% of this sample has ITA ≤ 10° and only about 15% has ITA > 28°. https://doaj.org/article/527e19f1c33a44bbb0f955b1c74d9e7c. The city is not stated in the abstract I retrieved.
- **Sarkar et al. 2019.** IJDVL 85(2):160–168, New Delhi. They recruited Indian phototype IV and V participants defined by cheek ITA: IV as 10–28°, V as −30° to 10°. This shows that ITA ≤ 10° participants are routinely enrolled in Indian dermatology trials. ITA values at baseline were not readable in the fetched text. https://ijdvl.com/a-randomized-study-to-evaluate-the-efficacy-and-effectiveness-of-two-sunscreen-formulations-on-indian-skin-types-iv-and-v-with-pigmentation-irregularities/
- **Hourblin et al. 2014.** IJDVL 80(5):395–401, doi:10.4103/0378-6323.140290. They measured 1,204 women in Chennai, Mumbai, Kolkata and Delhi with a Chroma Meter CR-400 at the cheek and inner arm. The paper reports L* and visual grades, not ITA. Mumbai was the lightest city (45% "wheatish"); Chennai was the darkest (38% brown, 36% dark brown). https://ijdvl.com/?p=7174
- **Gujarat or Surat.** I found no Gujarat- or Surat-specific ITA° data.

**Implication.** On facial (cheek) ITA, most Indian adults appear to fall at or below 10°. The darker stratum (ITA ≤ 10°) should therefore fill easily in Surat, especially through SVNIT's pan-India student and staff body. The stratum most likely to fill slowly is the lighter one (ITA > 28°), perhaps 15% of adults. The risk register in proposal §9.3 ("darker stratum fills slowly") probably has this backwards.

Two caveats:
1. ITA depends on the site measured: forehead and cheek read darker than the inner arm.
2. ITA also depends on the instrument. The pilot's own colorimeter distribution should set the quotas.

Consider revising the risk row to "The lighter stratum (ITA° > 28) fills slowly", or moving the stratum cut-points so that each holds roughly a third of the local distribution.

  Title (146 of 500 characters)

  SAMA: Skin-tone-equitable measurement of heart rate and heart-rate variability from
  facial video, towards camera-based care for Indian populations

  Project summary (2,016 of 3,000 characters)

  Video consultations now reach crores of Indians through eSanjeevani, yet they carry no
  vital signs. Remote photoplethysmography (rPPG) reads the pulse from tiny colour
  changes in facial skin using an ordinary webcam or phone, but the signal weakens in
  darker skin, dim light and compressed video: the same optical problem that made pulse
  oximeters miss low oxygen in darker-skinned patients. No Indian dataset measures skin
  tone objectively, records ECG-grade ground truth and tests the video compression that
  teleconsultations use. SAMA (Sanskrit: equal) will measure, explain, fix and guarantee
  camera-based heart rate (HR) and heart-rate variability (HRV) across Indian skin tones.
  WP1 records 120 adults in three colorimeter-measured skin-tone strata (Individual
  Typology Angle), balanced for gender, with 1 kHz ECG, four synchronised cameras, two
  light levels and a seven-level compression ladder, during rest, a mock teleconsultation,
  emotional film clips from the PI's Indian database (AFDI), mental stress and post-exercise
  recovery. WP2 audits 10 rPPG methods and splits each person's error into skin optics,
  light, device and compression, testing whether compression widens the skin-tone gap.
  WP3 develops the SAMA method: skin-tone-adaptive camera exposure, calibrated noise-
  weighted pulse extraction trained to minimise the worst-served group's error, and an
  equal-reliability gate that calibrates 90% intervals separately for each skin-tone stratum
  and withholds unreliable readings. WP4 tests HRV agreement with ECG in every stratum
  and releases an open toolkit and an on-device Android research demonstrator. Five pre-
  registered hypotheses each have a numeric success criterion and about 80% power or

  1. Origin of the proposal
  SAMA asks whether an ordinary phone or laptop camera can give every Indian, whatever
  their skin tone, a trustworthy heart rate and heart-rate variability (HRV), and it shows
  exactly where it cannot. The question grows out of the PI's own record:

        His M.Tech work measured heart rate from facial video in different user states (best
        paper, IHCI 2014).
        His PhD and postdoctoral work showed that cardiac and brain dynamics jointly predict
        emotional arousal in Indian viewers (Brain Sciences 2022), using the Affective Film
        Dataset from India (AFDI), which he built and validated (2023).
        His group has validated a low-cost physiological device against a laboratory reference
        system (Frontiers in Neuroscience 2025).

  Joining a Department of Artificial Intelligence turned this into a deployable question.
  Three facts make it urgent.

    1. Scale. eSanjeevani, India's national telemedicine service, passed 50 crore
       teleconsultations in October 2026. Each consultation has a camera but records no vital
       signs.
    2. A known equity failure. Optical sensors depend on how light passes through skin.
       Pulse oximeters missed hidden low oxygen almost three times as often in Black
       patients as in white patients (Sjoding et al. 2020). In the largest skin-tone-labelled
       rPPG study, camera heart-rate error was 6.2 beats a minute for Fitzpatrick types V–VI
       against 3.9 for types I–II (Chari et al. 2020), and darker skin is under-represented in
       public rPPG datasets (Bondarenko et al. 2025).
    3. India is missing. The two Indian rPPG datasets we found record heart rate only, against
       a finger oximeter, in 44 adults (Dasari et al. 2021), and respiration only, in 119 videos
       (Saikia et al. 2026). Neither measures skin tone with an instrument, uses ECG, or tests
       video compression.

  Scope. SAMA is a measurement-science project in healthy adults. It produces the
  evidence and open tools that any later clinical validation or platform decision would need.
  It builds no medical device and claims no deployment.

  2. Status of research and the gap

  2.1 International status
  Methods. Remote photoplethysmography (rPPG) recovers the pulse from colour changes
  in facial skin (Verkruysse et al. 2008). Signal-processing methods separate pulse from
  noise by blind source separation (Poh et al. 2010) or skin-reflectance models (CHROM, de
  Haan and Jeanne 2013; POS, Wang et al. 2017). Deep networks followed (DeepPhys,
  PhysNet, TS-CAN, PhysFormer, EfficientPhys). The open rPPG-Toolbox (Liu et al. 2023)
  now benchmarks them on common footing. Its tables show the field's core weakness:
  trained networks reach about 1 beat a minute on clean laboratory video but 12–17 on the
  harder MMPD set.

  Skin tone. A meta-analysis found larger errors for darker skin (Nowara et al. 2020). On the
  VITAL dataset, error rose from 3.9 to 6.2 beats a minute between Fitzpatrick types I–II and
  V–VI (Chari et al. 2020). Their optical analysis traced the bias to camera noise, not
  biophysics: melanin leaves the pulse's relative size almost unchanged but lowers the light



                                                                                        Page 4 of 31
SAMA · Final proposal




  reaching the sensor. MMPD (Tang et al. 2023) reported errors above 10 beats a minute for
  most unsupervised methods on Fitzpatrick types IV–VI. Skin tone is almost always
  labelled by eye on the Fitzpatrick scale, which annotators apply inconsistently (Barrett et
  al. 2023).

  Compression. Video compression degrades rPPG (McDuff et al. 2017), and networks have
  been built for compressed video (Yu et al. 2019). Whether compression widens the skin-
  tone gap has not been tested on recorded people. The only study we found is an
  unreviewed 2026 simulation (TRACE-rPPG): compression did not widen the gap in mean
  error, because darker skin was already near failure, while signal fidelity fell far more for
  darker skin.

  HRV. HRV needs individual beats, which rPPG recovers less reliably. Against a reference
  sensor, video-derived RMSSD correlated only r = 0.53 (Finžgar and Podržaj 2020). A 2026
  pilot found r = 0.88 for lnRMSSD at rest, but in 35 white participants only, and reported no
  ICC.

  Reliability. Recent work estimates rPPG uncertainty with Bayesian networks (Ma and
  Chen 2025) or corrects implausible heart rates (Tian et al. 2026), but neither reports
  reliability by skin tone. A 2026 metrology guide for PPG recommends conformal
  prediction (Harris et al. 2026), and statistics offers intervals with equal coverage for every
  group (Romano et al. 2020). We found no rPPG study that guarantees equal reliability
  across skin tones.


  2.2 National status
  Dasari et al. (2021) recorded 44 Indian adults outdoors on a smartphone against a finger
  oximeter: heart rate only, wide error, no measured skin tone. Saikia, Gupta and colleagues
  (IIT Indore, 2026) released 119 videos for respiratory rate and showed that skin-tone-
  aware channel weighting helps, with weaker results at both ends of the skin-tone range
  for lack of samples. The PI's video heart-rate (2014) and cardiac–emotion (2022) studies
  are the closest Indian work on affective cardiac sensing. No Indian group, to our
  knowledge, has released an ECG-referenced, skin-tone-measured rPPG dataset.

  SAMA adds five things, each new to our knowledge:

    1. Skin tone measured with an instrument (colorimeter ITA°), not judged by eye, in an
       Indian sample.
    2. An error budget. A lossless reference camera, synchronised consumer cameras, two
       light levels and an offline compression ladder let each person's error be split into skin
       optics, light, device and codec.
    3. The first real-data test of whether compression widens the skin-tone gap, with two
       competing predictions set out in advance (Figure 2).
    4. Fairness at capture, not only in the algorithm: skin-tone-adaptive exposure tested
       against auto-exposure on the same phone model.
    5. An equal-reliability guarantee: intervals calibrated separately for each skin-tone
       stratum, with abstention rates audited per stratum, and HRV judged against 1 kHz ECG
       in every stratum.

  3. Research questions and pre-registered hypotheses
  SAMA answers four questions:

    1. How large is the skin-tone gradient in camera heart-rate and HRV error among Indians,
       and how much of it comes from light, device and compression?
    2. Does video compression widen that gradient, or does darker-skin error already sit at a
       floor?
    3. Can changes at capture and in the algorithm close the gap, with equal reliability in
       every skin-tone group?
    4. Is camera-derived HRV valid enough for stress assessment in every skin-tone group?

  Each hypothesis has one primary endpoint, one test and a numeric success criterion,
  registered on the Open Science Framework before the first participant is recorded. Skin-
  tone strata are fixed in advance from Individual Typology Angle (ITA°) categories (Del Bino
  and Bernerd 2013): lighter, ITA° above 28; middle, 10 to 28; darker, 10 or below. The primary

  Four work packages run as measure, explain, fix and guarantee (Figure 3). Recording ends

  package ends in a deliverable that stands on its own, so a delay in one does not void the

  Session protocol (Figure 4). About 80 minutes, of which 47 are recorded on every stream
  at once. Each half of the session repeats the same blocks under one light level, about 500
  lux diffuse LED or about 100 lux overhead light, so every condition is measured within the
  same person under both. Light order, the assignment of the four AFDI clips (two neutral,
  two high-arousal) and which phone unit is adaptive are counterbalanced. In the mock
  teleconsultation, a researcher on a video link asks ten scripted history-taking questions
  (symptoms, medicines, sleep) and the participant answers naturally. A one-minute step
  test followed by three seated minutes gives each person a falling heart rate from about
  the range seen with fever or anxiety. Participants are paid ₹750.

  Compression ladder. The lossless reference video is resized to 720p at 30 fps and re-
  encoded with ffmpeg: H.264 at 2,000, 1,000, 500 and 250 kbps, and VP8 (the codec
  WebRTC video calls must support) at 500 and 250 kbps. With the lossless original this

  Quality control. Every month the team checks ECG quality, synchronisation and quota
  progress. A session is re-recorded if more than 20% of its ECG is unusable.

  EfficientPhys are included because they run on phones. Each neural model is tested

  twice: as released (pretrained on PURE, UBFC-rPPG or SCAMPS) and fine-tuned on
  SAMA-120 by five-fold cross-validation split by participant, with every stratum
  represented in every fold.

  Metrics. In 30-second windows, overlapping by 15 seconds:

        heart rate: MAE, RMSE, Pearson r, signal-to-noise ratio (dB), and the share of windows
        within the heart-rate-monitor tolerance of ±5 beats a minute or ±10%, whichever is
        greater (ANSI/AAMI EC13);
        beats, in 60-second windows: inter-beat-interval error (ms), and RMSSD and SDNN
        error;
        fairness: worst-stratum MAE, and the gap and ratio between the worst and best strata.

  Error budget. For participant i, block b and each combination of light, device and codec, a
  linear mixed model splits the error into parts:

     \log(1+e_{ib\ell dc}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\mathrm{ITA}_i + \textstyle\sum_k
     \gamma_k X_k + \sum_k \delta_k\,\mathrm{ITA}_i X_k +
     \boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{z}_{ib} + u_i + v_b + \varepsilon_{ib\ell
     dc}


  Here e is the window's absolute heart-rate error. X_k are indicators for dim light, each
  consumer device and each codec level, so γ_k is a component's average cost and δ_k how
  that cost changes with skin tone. z holds head motion (from face landmarks), age, sex,
  beard and spectacles; u_i and v_b are random effects for participant and block type. The
  log handles the skew of errors. Each stratum's budget, the predicted error on lossless
  video in good light plus the increment from each component, is shown as a stacked bar
  with participant-bootstrap confidence intervals.

  H1 analysis. The primary test regresses each participant's mean MAE (POS, lossless
  reference video, both lights, all seated blocks) on ITA°, one-sided. The mixed model is the
  sensitivity analysis. Secondary analyses repeat this for the other nine methods with Holm
  correction, and replace ITA° with Monk and Fitzpatrick ratings to show what visual labels
  miss.

  H2 analysis. Each participant's compression penalty, MAE at 500 kbps H.264 minus
  lossless MAE, is regressed on ITA°, two-sided. Secondary analyses treat the whole ladder
  as a dose–response (ITA° × log bitrate), repeat it for VP8 and for signal-to-noise ratio, and
  compare the live-call recordings with offline 500 kbps encoding to check that the ladder
  predicts a real call.

  WP3 · The SAMA method (months 9–20)
  Chari et al. (2020) traced the skin-tone gap to too little pulse signal above camera noise.
  SAMA attacks that cause at three points: at capture, during extraction, and when
  deciding whether to report a number (Figure 5). Each step below is specified well enough
  to be re-implemented from this text.




  Figure 5. The SAMA method. Steps 1, 4 and 6 are new; steps 2, 3 and 5 adapt established
  methods.

  Step 1. Skin-tone-adaptive exposure (new). Phone auto-exposure is tuned for a pleasing
  picture, not for the pulse, and can leave a darker face low in the sensor's range, where
  noise and 8-bit rounding swamp the pulse. Before each block, a 10-second calibration
  measures the face's mean brightness. Exposure time (capped at 1/30 s to keep 30 frames
  a second) and then sensor gain are raised until the face reaches a target share of full
  scale, with under 1% of skin pixels clipped. White balance is locked from the colour chart.
  The target is fixed from the pilot. H3 tests this rule against default auto-exposure on the
  same phone model.




                                                                                         Page 15 of 31
SAMA · Final proposal




  Step 2. Calibration and a measured noise model. The 24-patch colour chart gives each
  camera's mapping to linear RGB. Static frames of the chart give the camera's noise
  variance at every brightness level (the photon-transfer method), so the expected noise of
  each skin patch is known rather than guessed.

  Step 3. Noise-weighted skin patches. A face mesh places about 30 patches on the
  forehead and cheeks. Eyes, mouth, beard, bindi and specular highlights are masked. Each
  patch is weighted by its pulse-band power divided by its expected noise variance. This
  extends the signal-to-noise weighting of Chari et al. (2020) with a measured, camera-
  specific noise model.

  Step 4. Physics-guided, worst-group-trained extraction (new). POS projection of the
  weighted linear RGB gives a candidate pulse. A small one-dimensional convolutional
  network (under 0.5 million parameters, so it runs on a phone) refines it from the patch
  traces. Feature-wise linear modulation (Perez et al. 2018) lets the network use the
  measured ITA° and face brightness. The loss combines negative Pearson correlation with
  a spectral heart-rate term. Training uses group distributionally robust optimisation
  (Sagawa et al. 2020) over six groups, three strata by two light levels, so the network
  minimises the worst group's loss rather than the average.

  Step 5. Sub-frame beat timing. After band-pass filtering (0.7–3.0 Hz), pulse peaks are
  located by parabolic interpolation to 1 ms. Inter-beat intervals pass the same artefact
  rules as the ECG, then give heart rate, RMSSD and SDNN.

  Step 6. Equal-reliability gate (new). Group-conditional (Mondrian) split conformal
  prediction (Vovk et al. 2005; Romano et al. 2020) gives each window a 90% interval for
  heart rate, calibrated separately for each skin-tone stratum on participants never used in
  training:

     s_j=\frac{|\hat h_j-h_j|}{\hat\sigma(x_j)},\qquad \hat q_g=\text{the
     }\left\lceil 0.9\,(n_g+1)\right\rceil\text{-th smallest } s_j \text{ in
     stratum } g,\qquad C(x)=\hat h\pm\hat q_g\,\hat\sigma(x)


  Here ĥ is the camera estimate, h the ECG value, and σ̂ (x) a difficulty score learned from
  patch signal-to-noise, motion and brightness. A reading is shown only if the interval's half-
  width is at most 5 beats a minute; otherwise the system says "no reliable reading".
  Because windows from one person are correlated, calibration draws one window per
  participant and repeats the draw 200 times. Reported per stratum: coverage, interval
  width, abstention rate and useful yield (windows shown and within ±5 beats a minute).

  Training and validation. Pretraining uses public datasets (PURE, UBFC-rPPG, UBFC-Phys
  and the synthetic SCAMPS). Fine-tuning on SAMA-120 uses nested cross-validation split
  by participant: five outer folds for testing, inner folds for settings. Ablations remove each

  Measure before you compress. Steps 2–6 also run on the compressed ladder, so the
  project reports, per stratum, how much accuracy is kept by measuring on the phone
  before video is compressed for the call.

  H3 and H4 analyses follow section 3. The best baseline for H4 is fixed as the baseline with
  the lowest darker-stratum MAE in the same outer folds.

  Deliverables. D3 method paper, open model and code, month 20.


  WP4 · HRV, stress and release (months 13–24)
  HRV agreement (H5). For every segment of at least 60 seconds, RMSSD and SDNN are
  computed from ECG and from the camera, using both SAMA and the best baseline. The
  primary endpoint is RMSSD reactivity (log RMSSD in mental arithmetic minus the
  preceding rest) on lossless reference video in good light. Agreement is judged by ICC(2,1),
  absolute agreement, against the bands of Koo and Li (2016), with Bland–Altman limits for
  each stratum. Secondary analyses cover the other lights, devices and codec levels; the
  paced-breathing block tests beat recovery when HRV is large. The respiration belt shows
  whether apparent HRV changes are driven by breathing.

  Stress and arousal (E1, exploratory). Leave-one-subject-out classifiers (logistic
  regression and gradient boosting on heart rate, RMSSD, SDNN and pulse amplitude)
  separate arithmetic from rest and high- from low-arousal clips. Each is trained once on
  ECG features and once on camera features, and accuracy is compared per stratum with
  bootstrap intervals. Skin conductance gives an independent arousal check. This links
  SAMA to tele-mental-health, where Tele-MANAS has begun adding video consultations in
  some states, without a confirmatory claim.

  On-device research demonstrator. The SAMA model is converted to TensorFlow Lite and
  runs steps 1–6 inside an Android app built on Open Camera's exposure control, so no
  video leaves the phone. It is tested for latency, battery use and abstention on the two
  study phones and one entry-level phone, using recorded SAMA sessions and short live
  sessions with 10 lab volunteers under the same ethics approval. It is a research
  demonstrator: not a medical device and not for diagnosis.

  Justification.

        ECG at 1,000 Hz is needed because HRV ground truth depends on beat timing to the
        millisecond; two BITalino kits let two sessions run in parallel. The Polar H10 sensors are
        a validated second reference and a backup.

SAMA · Final proposal




        Four cameras are the experiment. The machine-vision camera gives the best-case
        signal and the lossless source for the compression ladder. The webcams and phones
        give what patients actually use. Two units of one phone model isolate the effect of
        exposure (H3); the entry-level phone tests the demonstrator on a budget device.
        The colorimeter measures skin tone with an instrument, the basis of every skin-tone
        claim; it also checks camera-derived ITA°.
        Controlled light is needed to test light as a cause; the lux meter makes it a measured
        covariate.
        The workstation writes several video streams losslessly in real time (about 1 GB a
        minute, roughly 6 TB over the study). Backup drives keep raw faces off any cloud.
        Software is all open source: rPPG-Toolbox, Open Camera, Jitsi Meet, ffmpeg, Lab
